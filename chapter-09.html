Chapter 9 of 10

When the Evidence Changes, Change Your Mind

The Bravest Words in Your Language

Of all the habits I have watched across your history, one has done more good than any weapon or empire: the willingness to say "I was wrong." Every leap in your medicine, your science, and your freedom began with someone abandoning a belief the evidence no longer supported.

Yet modern America treats changing one's mind as a weakness. Politicians who update their views are mocked as flip-floppers. People admire the man who "sticks to his guns," even when the guns are pointed at his own foot.

From where I stand, this is backward. Refusing to change your mind is not loyalty to truth. It is loyalty to an earlier version of yourself, who formed the opinion with less information than you have now. The principle is simple: hold beliefs in proportion to the evidence, and when the evidence changes, change with it.

What Your Scientists Have Found

Your psychologists call this quality intellectual humility: an awareness that one's beliefs might be wrong. It does not mean having no opinions. It means holding them with an open hand.

In 2017, Duke University psychologist Mark Leary and colleagues ran four studies on the trait (Duke University). They found:

  • The humble judge evidence better. They were better at telling a strong, fact-based argument from a weak one.
  • The humble judge people more fairly. After reading essays for and against religion, they were less likely to condemn a writer's character because of the writer's views, the very standard Chapter 4 asked of you.
  • The humble forgive the "flip-flopper." They were less likely to condemn a politician who changed position because of new evidence.

The researchers found essentially no difference in intellectual humility between liberals and conservatives, or between religious and nonreligious people. Humility belongs to no American tribe. Neither does arrogance.

Why Humans Dig In

Beliefs become identity. Once a view marks you as a member of a team, giving it up feels like betrayal. The mind starts defending the tribe instead of weighing the facts. I have watched this pattern in every human civilization.

Sunk costs. If you have argued for something for twenty years, admitting error seems to waste those years. But the years are gone either way. The only question is whether you spend the next twenty defending a mistake.

Pride disguised as principle. Many of you would rather be consistent than correct, because consistency looks like strength. It is not.

Here is a hopeful example. A famous 2010 study by Brendan Nyhan and Jason Reifler reported a "backfire effect": correcting some people's false political beliefs seemed to make them believe harder. In 2019, Thomas Wood and Ethan Porter tested more than 10,000 people on 52 issues and found no correction that triggered backfire (Wood & Porter, Political Behavior). Across the political spectrum, people moved toward the facts. And Nyhan and Reifler accepted the new evidence, joined Wood and Porter in further research, and conceded the effect may have been overstated (Nieman Lab). The researchers changed their minds. Your species can.

Strong Minds That Bent

The doctors who washed their hands, eventually. In 1847, Hungarian physician Ignaz Semmelweis required doctors in his Vienna maternity ward to wash their hands, and deaths from childbed fever fell from about 18% to about 1% (Britannica). Most leading obstetricians rejected his findings anyway; historians believe one reason was that his theory blamed doctors for their patients' deaths. The Vienna hospital even abandoned mandatory handwashing (National Geographic). Stubbornness is never free. Someone always pays.

The ulcer that wasn't stress. For decades, your doctors believed ulcers came from stress and diet. In 1982, Australians Barry Marshall and Robin Warren identified a bacterium as the real cause, and were met with great skepticism (Nobel Prize ceremony speech). Marshall later recalled that for about ten years nobody believed them (NBC News). He drank a culture of the bacteria to prove it. They received the 2005 Nobel Prize, and peptic ulcer disease became a condition that can be permanently cured (NobelPrize.org).

The ground beneath your feet. When Alfred Wegener proposed in 1912 that the continents drift, geologists ridiculed him, and for decades warned young scientists away from the idea (Smithsonian). Exploration of the ocean floor from the 1950s revived it, and by the late 1960s plate tectonics was accepted by almost all geologists (UC Berkeley Museum of Paleontology).

Six Habits of a Mind That Can Move

Leary and his colleagues believe intellectual humility can be taught. Here is how to practice it.

  1. Name your price. For any belief you care about, ask what evidence would change your mind. If the answer is "nothing," you hold a creed, not a conclusion.
  2. Rate your confidence. Say "I'm about 70% sure." It is easier to move from 70 to 50 than to swallow a full reversal.
  3. Argue the other side first. State the opposing view so well its believers would agree with your summary. Then critique it.
  4. Read one source you distrust each week, to find where your own side is weakest.
  5. Separate the claim from the claimant. A bad person can be right; a good person can be wrong.
  6. Praise the change. When someone admits a mistake, thank them. Punishing honesty only teaches people to hide.

This Book Must Pass Its Own Test

I would be a hypocrite to exempt myself. In Chapter 4 I argued that belief is a choice, yet your data show most Americans keep the faith they were raised in, which is why I narrowed my case to conduct. In Chapter 3 I warned that intelligence is declining, but the key study was an online sample whose own author urged caution. In Chapter 7 I blamed government for tilting the scale; critics argue that technology and global trade explain more of the gap than any law.

I still believe each chapter points at something true. But I hold them at 70%, not 100%. Bring me better evidence, and I owe you a better opinion. The weak mind cannot afford to be wrong. The strong mind cannot afford to stay wrong.
