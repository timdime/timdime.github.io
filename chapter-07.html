Chapter 7 of 10

Who Rigged the Scale?

The Referee Is Not Neutral

Across your history I have watched every civilization argue about who gets what. Kings, priests, landlords, merchants, and parliaments have all claimed the right to decide. Americans like to believe their contest is simpler: work hard, save, invest, and you rise. There is truth in that. But there is a player most Americans forget to watch: their own government.

Washington writes the tax code. It sets interest rates through the Federal Reserve. It decides which investments get special treatment. Every one of those choices moves money from some pockets to others. A referee who also writes the rules is not neutral, no matter which team holds the whistle.

This chapter asks two questions. How has government helped widen the gap between the very wealthy and everyone else? And what do America's two parties actually want for your personal wealth? I will present each party's case as its own supporters would, and let you judge.

The Size of the Gap

The Federal Reserve tracks who owns America's wealth every quarter. As of mid-2026, the richest 1% of households held 32.5% of the nation's net worth (Federal Reserve, via FRED). The entire bottom half held about 2.5% (Federal Reserve, via FRED). The average household in the top 1% owns as much as about 650 households in the bottom half combined. And the bottom half is fragile: its total net worth fell from about $1.4 trillion in 2000 to under $0.3 trillion by 2010 (Federal Reserve, via FRED).

Every society I have observed has had some gap. People differ in talent, effort, and luck. The narrower question is how much of this gap government helped build.

Four Ways the Government Tilts the Field

None of this is a conspiracy. Most of it passed with good intentions, often with votes from both parties.

1. It taxes owning more gently than working. Long-term capital gains face a top federal rate of 20%, while wages and short-term gains face a top rate of 37% (Congressional Research Service). High earners also owe an additional 3.8% tax on investment income (Congressional Research Service), while their wages carry Medicare payroll taxes. A high-earning surgeon pays a higher top rate on her paycheck than an investor pays on his stock gains.

2. It lets gains pass untaxed at death. Under the "step-up in basis" rule, when someone dies, the tax basis of their assets resets to market value, so gains built up over a lifetime are never taxed as income. Imagine a stock bought for $1 million and worth $50 million at death: the heirs receive it with no income tax ever paid on the $49 million gain.

3. Its central bank lifts asset prices, with mixed effects. After the 2008 crisis, the Federal Reserve held interest rates near zero and bought large amounts of bonds, a policy called quantitative easing. Critics say low rates widen inequality by boosting the wealth of those who already own stocks (Route Fifty). The research is more mixed. A 2024 New York Fed study found quantitative easing widened the gap between the top 10% and everyone else by lifting profits and stock prices, while narrowing gaps within the bottom 90% by lowering unemployment (Federal Reserve Bank of New York). Easy money also lifted home prices, and real estate is spread far more evenly than stocks: the wealthiest 10% own only about 45% of household real estate, according to the Fed. I count this a partial tilt, not a clear one.

4. It makes housing scarce. Local zoning and land-use rules limit how many homes can be built. Economists Edward Glaeser and Joseph Gyourko found that in the most expensive markets, especially New York City and California, these rules, not just high demand, explain why prices far exceed building costs (Wharton). Scarcity rewards existing owners and puts the first home out of reach for the young.

The Republican Approach: Let People Keep It

The goal, as Republicans describe it. Wealth is created by individuals and businesses, not by government. Tax less, regulate less, and the economy grows. Money left in private hands is invested and creates jobs. Taxing savings, investment, and inheritance punishes behavior a healthy country should reward, and threatens family farms and businesses passed between generations.

The record. The party's signature law of this decade is the One Big Beautiful Bill Act, signed July 4, 2025 (IRS). It made the 2017 income tax rates permanent and added:

  • Deductions for tip income and overtime pay, capped at $12,500 for individuals and $25,000 for couples (Kiplinger)
  • A child tax credit of $2,200 per child
  • "Trump accounts," savings accounts seeded with a $1,000 federal deposit for children born 2025 through 2028
  • An estate tax exemption raised permanently to $15 million per person, $30 million per couple

What the scorekeepers found. Congress's nonpartisan budget office estimated the law, on average, takes about $1,200 a year from households in the lowest tenth of incomes and adds about $13,600 a year to households in the top tenth, largely because of cuts to Medicaid and food assistance (Thomson Reuters on CBO). Middle-income households came out modestly ahead. Critics note the CBO estimated the law adds about $3.4 trillion to deficits over a decade (Bloomberg Tax). Supporters note that measured against simply extending the 2017 tax cuts, the CBO found it would reduce deficits by $366 billion (Accounting Today, via Bloomberg), and that static estimates leave out growth.

The Democratic Approach: Spread It Wider

The goal, as Democrats describe it. Extreme concentration of wealth is unfair and distorts democracy. The rules already tilt toward capital, so government should tilt them back: tax the very top more, and lower the costs that keep families from building wealth.

The record. In 2021 Democrats temporarily expanded the child tax credit, and child poverty fell to a record low of 5.2% that year before the expansion lapsed (U.S. Census Bureau). House Democrats have floated restoring a larger child tax credit, making health care subsidies permanent, and ending President Trump's tariffs (Bloomberg Government). Senators Cory Booker and Chris Van Hollen proposed cutting taxes for low- and middle-income workers while raising them on the wealthy (Bloomberg Tax); Van Hollen's plan would end federal income tax for people earning under $46,000, or $92,000 for couples (The Fiscal Times).

A party divided on method. Some progressives would rather expand the safety net than cut taxes at all. Centrist House Democrats have built an agenda around paid family and medical leave, a bigger child tax credit, extended Affordable Care Act subsidies, a higher minimum wage, and rolling back tariffs (Spectrum News).

What critics say. Republicans argue that heavy pandemic-era spending under Democratic leadership helped fuel the 2021-2022 inflation surge, which hit lower-income families hardest. Even some analysts on the left have questioned the cost of the new tax-cut plans. Democrats reply that the inflation was worldwide and driven largely by pandemic supply shocks.

Side by Side

Question Republicans Democrats
Where does wealth come from? Private effort, investment, and enterprise Effort, plus public investment and rules that shape who keeps the gains
Main tool Lower taxes and lighter regulation Higher taxes at the top, targeted credits and services below
Taxes on investment and inheritance Keep low; estate exemption raised to $15 million Raise taxes on the very wealthy
Safety-net programs Trim and add work requirements Expand, especially child care and health coverage
Help for working families Deductions for tips and overtime, $2,200 child credit, Trump accounts Bigger child credit; income tax cuts for lower earners
Biggest risk, per critics Rewards those already wealthy; adds to the debt Raises spending and costs; may slow investment

Where they quietly agree. Both parties now court workers with tax breaks; many Democrats embraced the tips and overtime deductions. Neither has ended the step-up in basis at death, though Joe Biden proposed changing it (Congressional Research Service). Several forces widening the gap carry fingerprints from both sides.

Follow the Rules, Not the Slogans

I have watched rulers of every era promise that their rules served the common people. Some did. Many served the rulers first. Americans should listen less to the speeches and more to the fine print: who gets the deduction, who loses the benefit, who is quietly exempted.

Ask three questions of any economic promise. Does it reward working, or only owning? Does it help someone build an asset, or only spend? And who pays for it, now or through debt later?

The scale may be tilted, but it was tilted by laws, and laws are written by people Americans elect. Judge them by what they do with the scale, not by the jersey they wear.
