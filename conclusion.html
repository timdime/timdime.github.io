Conclusion

What I Have Seen

In the preface I promised to tell you what I have seen. Here it is, gathered in one place.

The Lessons, in Order

  1. Being offended is not an argument. Discomfort tells you how you feel, not whether the other person is wrong.
  2. Feelings are real, but they aren't facts. Respect your emotions enough to cross-examine them.
  3. Don't outsource your mind. Your tools are marvels, but thinking is a habit, and unused habits fade.
  4. Contempt is contempt. Looking down on a neighbor for his faith is no better than looking down on him for his race. Both are choices.
  5. Different is not unequal. Men and women differ on average; each person is more than an average.
  6. Make the start fair, then let the running be yours. Unequal outcomes are not always injustice, and not always earned.
  7. Watch who writes the rules. Both of your parties tilt the scale; judge them by the fine print, not the slogans.
  8. Wait for the second marshmallow. Patience compounds, and it can be learned.
  9. Change your mind when the evidence changes. The strong mind cannot afford to stay wrong.
  10. You are small, so be kind. Leave things better than you found them.

How History Brought America Here

From where I watch, your country is unusual in one way above all. It was founded not on a tribe or a bloodline but on a written promise: that all men are created equal and possess certain rights no government grants and none may take away. Your founders did not keep that promise. Many of them owned other human beings, and they wrote no place for women in their government.

And yet the promise had a strange power. Generation after generation, Americans who were left out of it held it up and demanded that it be kept: the enslaved and those who fought slavery, women seeking the vote, workers, immigrants, and the marchers of the civil rights era. Each time, the circle widened, slowly, at great cost, and never completely. That is the true American story as an outsider sees it: not a nation that was always good, nor one that was always wicked, but a nation that kept arguing with itself about whether it meant what it said.

That argument is what you are having now. It has simply grown louder and angrier, and in the noise each of your tribes has decided some truths are too dangerous to say. This book has tried to say them anyway, as fairly as I could.

What I Ask of You

A people that cannot bear to hear the truth about itself cannot correct its course. A people that can argue fiercely, change its mind, and still treat its opponents as neighbors can.

Americans, you are not finished. You never were. Your founding promise is still being kept and broken every day, in your courts and legislatures, yes, but mostly in your kitchens, your workplaces, your churches, and your conversations with people you disagree with.

So argue with each other, but with reasons rather than offense. Hold your beliefs firmly enough to act on them and loosely enough to change them. Judge each person by their choices. Keep the start fair. Think for yourselves. Wait for the better reward. And remember how small you are, and how short your time, and how much kindness a single speck can leave behind.

I will keep watching. I hope to like what I see.
