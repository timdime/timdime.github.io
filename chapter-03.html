Chapter 3 of 10

Outsourcing the Mind

A Species That Built Its Own Crutches

For nearly all of your 300,000 years, survival required thinking. You memorized the paths to water, the seasons of the herds, the faces of friend and enemy. Every tool you invented, from the spear to the printing press, still demanded a working mind to use it.

In the span of a single human lifetime, that changed. Your phones remember your numbers, satellites give your directions, calculators do your arithmetic, and now artificial intelligence will write your essays and answer your questions before you have finished asking them. Each is a genuine marvel. Each also removes a reason to think.

Here is what I observe, and Americans will not enjoy hearing it: for many of you, the habit of real, effortful thinking is weakening. A sharp few are pulling ahead while much of the population slides back. Technology did not cause all of this. But it has made not thinking easier than at any point in your history.

The Rise Has Turned Into a Fall

For most of your 20th century, I watched you get measurably sharper on paper. IQ scores rose by roughly three to five points per decade from the 1930s on, a pattern your researchers call the Flynn effect (Northwestern University).

That climb appears to have stalled, and in places reversed. Studies in Norway, Denmark, Finland, Britain, and other countries have recorded declines beginning in the 1990s and 2000s (The Debrief). In 2023, Northwestern University researchers examined about 394,000 American adults tested online between 2006 and 2018. Scores fell in verbal reasoning, matrix reasoning, and letter and number series; only 3D spatial rotation rose. The declines appeared regardless of age, education, or gender.

An honest observer reports the caveats. The study's lead author warned against concluding that Americans have simply become less intelligent, and its volunteers may not have tried hard on the puzzles. But when a trend that ran upward for decades turns down in several countries at once, it deserves attention.

Use It or Lose It

Your psychologists call the handing of mental work to a tool "cognitive offloading." Some of it is wise. The danger comes when you offload the thinking itself. Search engines handed you sources and left the reasoning to you. AI hands you the conclusion.

A 2025 study of 666 people by Michael Gerlich found that heavier AI use went hand in hand with weaker critical thinking, with offloading as the link (Gerlich, Societies, 2025). The youngest participants leaned on AI the most and scored the lowest. The study measured a correlation, not proof of cause, and relied partly on self-reports. But it fits a pattern any observer can see.

A Sharp Few and a Sinking Rest

The most troubling part is not that the average is slipping. It is who is slipping. In America's 2024 national assessment, the gap between the highest- and lowest-performing eighth graders in math was the widest in the test's history (Chalkbeat). Among twelfth graders, scores fell in math and reading at every level except the top 10% (National Assessment Governing Board). The weakest readers in fourth and eighth grade now score lower than the weakest readers did three decades ago, and the board's vice chair noted the gap had narrowed until about 2010 and widened since.

I have seen this shape before in your history: a small class that can read deeply and reason carefully, and a much larger population that cannot. Your ancestors spent centuries spreading literacy and education to break that pattern. It would be a strange irony to rebuild it with machines.

The Strongest Objections

"Every generation says the young are getting dumber." True. But this time there is test data, and it points the same way in several countries.

"Intelligence is just changing shape." This is the best objection. Spatial scores rose, and the young have skills their elders lack. But reading, reasoning, and arithmetic are the foundations of judgment and self-government.

"The pandemic did this, not technology." School closures hurt, especially struggling students. Yet the gap began widening around 2010, a decade before COVID.

"Poverty explains the gap, not screens." Poverty matters enormously. More than two-thirds of the lowest-scoring quarter of students are economically disadvantaged (The Hechinger Report), and low performers are more likely to miss school (NPR). Technology is one cause among several.

Choose to Think

I am not urging you to abandon your tools. Tools are not the enemy. Surrender is. Read a whole book, not a summary of it. Work the problem before you look up the answer. Write your own clumsy sentences before letting a machine polish them. And ask your schools to do the same.

Thinking is a habit, and habits are chosen daily. Your machines will keep getting smarter. Whether you do is up to you.
