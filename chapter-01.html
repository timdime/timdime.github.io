Being Offended Is Not an Argument

The New Trump Card

For most of your history, when one human disagreed with another, a reason was expected: a fact, an argument, a counterexample. In modern America I observe a shortcut spreading through the culture: "I'm offended." Those two words now end more conversations than most arguments could.

No single American tribe owns this card. A student demands a speaker be disinvited because his views are offensive. A parent demands a book be pulled from the library because its contents are offensive. A commentator calls an athlete's protest offensive to the flag. Each treats personal discomfort as the end of the matter. Each makes the same mistake.

The mistake is simple: offense describes how you feel. It says nothing about whether the other person is right.

Why Offense Proves Nothing

True things offend. In Chapter 9 you will meet a physician who insisted doctors wash their hands, and colleagues who rejected him partly because his finding blamed them. If offense disproved a claim, much of your progress would have been "disproved" the day it was spoken. The sting of a statement and its truth are measured on different scales.

False things comfort. If ideas are judged by how good they make you feel, flattery is rewarded and honesty punished. A society that does that slowly believes whatever is most pleasant, which is another way of saying it stops thinking, the danger Chapter 3 describes.

Offense is unlimited. Someone, somewhere, is offended by nearly anything worth saying. If offense is enough to silence a speaker, the most easily offended person in any room holds a veto over everyone else. This is sometimes called the heckler's veto. No free society should hand it to anyone.

Offense changes no one's mind. "I'm offended" may win a moment, but it persuades nobody. If you want to change a mind, Chapter 9 shows the way: reasons and evidence, offered to someone who feels respected enough to listen.

When Offense Is a Smoke Alarm

Fairness requires the other side. Offense is not an argument, but it can be a signal. A smoke alarm is not a fire, yet only a fool ignores it.

Sometimes people are offended because they have truly been insulted, demeaned, or lied about. A slur is not an idea to be debated; it is an attack. Mocking someone's grief is not brave honesty; it is cruelty. When someone is offended, the wise response is neither to obey nor to sneer, but to check for fire: was there a real wrong, or only a disagreement?

And those Americans who pride themselves on "telling it like it is" should hear this too: being offensive is not an argument either. Rudeness does not make a claim truer, any more than offense makes it false. Chapter 4 warns against contempt dressed as principle. That warning applies here too.

The goal is to separate two questions your culture constantly mixes up. Is it true? And was it said with decency? A mature person can answer yes to both.

Trade the Card for a Reason

When "I'm offended" rises in your throat, try one of these instead:

  • "I think that's wrong, and here's why."
  • "What's your evidence for that?"
  • "That felt like a personal attack rather than an argument. Did you mean it that way?"
  • "I disagree, but help me understand how you got there."

Each keeps the conversation alive and treats the other person as someone who can be reasoned with. Each also forces you to discover whether you have a better argument, or only a stronger feeling. That distinction is the subject of the next chapter.
