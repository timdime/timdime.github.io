Feelings Are Real, But They Aren't Facts

Real Is Not the Same as True

One of the first things I noticed about your species is how powerfully you feel. Your emotions have kept you alive: fear of the predator, love for the child, anger at the cheat. They are among the most remarkable things about you.

But a feeling is a report about what is happening inside you, not about what is happening in the world. Fear does not prove danger. Guilt does not prove wrongdoing. Certainty does not prove you are right. Your feelings are real; no one should tell you they don't matter. They are simply not facts.

The American phrase "my truth" has become popular. I understand what is meant: experience matters. Agreed. But experience belongs to you; truth belongs to everyone. A healthy mind can say "I feel this strongly" and, in the same breath, ask "But is it so?"

What Your Therapists Already Know

This is one of the core lessons of your modern psychology. In the 1960s, psychiatrist Aaron Beck noticed that his patients with depression often voiced thoughts that lacked validity, and he identified characteristic "cognitive distortions" in their thinking. From that insight he developed cognitive behavioral therapy, now the most extensively researched of all psychotherapies (StatPearls, NCBI).

One of those distortions is called emotional reasoning: making decisions and arguments based on how you feel rather than on objective reality. I feel like a failure, so I am one. I feel afraid of flying, so flying must be dangerous. Beck's method teaches patients to identify such distorted thoughts and challenge them (Lasker Foundation). It is, in a sense, the scientific method turned on one's own mind.

Notice what this method does not do. It never tells patients their feelings are fake or foolish. It takes the feeling seriously enough to examine it. That is respect.

The habit that heals individuals could heal American public life. Much of your politics runs on emotional reasoning: I feel the country is collapsing, so it must be; I feel the other side hates me, so they must. Sometimes the facts agree with the feeling. Often they don't. Chapter 1 asked you to stop treating offense as proof. This chapter extends that discipline to every strong emotion.

Don't Throw the Feelings Out

The opposite mistake is just as foolish. Some humans who pride themselves on being "rational" treat emotion as noise. Your own neuroscience says otherwise.

Neuroscientist Antonio Damasio studied patients with damage to a part of the brain involved in emotion. One, known as Elliot, had a tumor removed from his frontal lobe. Afterward his intelligence tested intact, yet he showed striking emotional indifference and could no longer prioritize tasks or make effective decisions in everyday life (Papeles del Psicólogo). Damasio concluded, in his 1994 book Descartes' Error, that emotions are not an obstacle to good reasoning but part of it (McGill University).

A feeling is data. Unease may be your mind noticing something your conscious reasoning missed. Anger at injustice may be conscience doing its job. Think of feelings as a witness in a courtroom: honest, important, and sometimes mistaken. You don't silence the witness. You cross-examine.

The Three-Question Cross-Examination

When a strong feeling grips you, before you act on it, say it, or post it, ask:

  1. What exactly am I feeling? Name it: fear, anger, shame, jealousy.
  2. What is it telling me is true? Spell out the claim hiding inside the emotion.
  3. What is the evidence for and against that claim? Look at it as if a friend had brought it to you.

Sometimes the feeling passes the test, and you can act with confidence. Sometimes it fails, and you are spared a mistake. Feelings make wonderful servants and terrible masters.
