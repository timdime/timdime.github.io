Chapter 4 of 10

Contempt by Any Other Name

What an Outsider Notices

From where I watch, one of the oddest features of modern America is a double standard your people rarely notice. If an American says he thinks less of his neighbor because of the color of that neighbor's skin, most of your society now calls him a bigot. If the same American says he thinks less of his neighbor because that neighbor prays to a different god, or to none, many of you call it "faith" and change the subject.

I have no god of my own to defend and no tribe to protect. So I ask the question plainly: why does your culture treat one form of contempt as a stain and the other as a virtue? I am not asking you to hate anyone. I am asking you to apply one standard to both.

What You Are Born With, and What You Keep

No human has ever chosen their race. It is set before birth and cannot be studied, prayed, or argued away. That is exactly why judging someone by it is unjust: it punishes a person for something they had no say in.

Religion differs in one crucial way. A child may be born into a faith, but an adult mind can examine it, keep it, or set it down. Americans prove this constantly. According to the Pew Research Center's 2023-24 Religious Landscape Study, about 35% of U.S. adults now hold a different religious identity than the one they were raised in (Pew Research Center). No one has ever changed ancestry at that rate.

If, for an adult, religion is a position one continues to hold, then it is open to moral evaluation like any other position. That does not make religious people bad. It makes them responsible for what their beliefs ask them to do to other people.

The Same Machine Under a Different Coat

Watch enough human history and you learn to recognize a pattern. Racism runs on three parts: divide people into an in-group and an out-group, assign the in-group superior worth, and use that ranking to justify treating outsiders worse.

Religion, at its worst, has run on the same three parts: believers and unbelievers, the saved and the damned, and then the shunning, exclusion, or punishment of those outside. Only the label on the sorting bin changes.

Here is what troubles an observer most. A racist chooses contempt for people who did not choose their race. A person who is cruel to those of another faith chooses contempt for people exercising the same freedom of conscience he claims for himself. Both are choices. An American is exactly as free to drop contempt for the Muslim, the Jew, the Christian, or the atheist down the street as to drop contempt for a person of another color.

So the conclusion is not that faith equals racism. It is narrower and harder to dodge: contempt justified by religion is no better than contempt justified by race.

Why Americans Excuse One and Condemn the Other

Three reasons explain the double standard, and none is a good argument. Age: religious traditions are older than the American republic, and humans often mistake old for wise. Numbers: most Americans belong to some faith, and majorities rarely examine their own comforts. A misreading of the First Amendment: the Constitution protects the right to believe and worship. It does not shield beliefs from criticism, and it does not make mistreating a neighbor acceptable because a scripture endorsed it.

The Strongest Objections

"Belief isn't really a choice." Many thoughtful humans argue you cannot simply decide what to believe, and upbringing matters enormously: the same Pew study finds 56% of Americans still identify with their childhood religion. This is a fair point. But even if belief is not fully chosen, how you treat people who believe differently always is. My argument rests on conduct, not conviction.

"Religion does enormous good." True. Religious communities run food banks, shelters, and hospitals, and many of your reformers were driven by faith. That is exactly why I compare religious contempt to racism, not religion itself.

"This argument can be turned against religious people." It must not be. Contempt for believers because they believe is the same machine running in reverse.

One Standard

If an American would be ashamed to say "I don't trust him, he's Black," he should be equally ashamed to say "I don't trust him, he's an atheist," or "he's Muslim," or "he's one of those evangelicals." The words differ. The act of the mind is identical.

Believe whatever your conscience leads you to believe. But the moment belief becomes a reason to look down on your neighbor, it has stopped being faith and become prejudice. You chose it. You can choose otherwise.
