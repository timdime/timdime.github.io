A Fair Start, Not a Tied Finish

A Fair Race Still Has a Winner

Picture a footrace in which every runner starts on the same line, wears the same shoes, and runs the same distance. The race is perfectly fair. It will still not end in a tie. Someone trained harder. Someone has longer legs. Someone stumbled. Someone didn't care to win.

That image holds this whole chapter. Equal opportunity means a fair start and fair rules. Equal outcomes means everyone crosses the line together. The first is a moral goal worth fighting for. The second cannot be achieved among free people without someone holding the fast runners back.

American public argument often confuses the two. A gap in results, by itself, does not prove the race was fixed. Sometimes it was. The task is telling which.

The Brothers Test

If you want to know whether unequal outcomes always mean unequal opportunity, look at brothers. Two boys raised in the same house, by the same parents, with the same schools have about as equal a start as life offers. Do they end up with the same income?

Not close. Economist Bhashkar Mazumder of the Chicago Federal Reserve estimated the correlation between American siblings' earnings and family income at roughly 0.45 to 0.5 (Federal Reserve Bank of Chicago). In plain words, family and community explain about half of why earnings differ. The other half reflects differences between people who shared nearly everything.

The Nordic countries, which invest heavily in equalizing opportunity, offer a striking result. There, brothers' earnings are less alike than in America, with correlations around 0.15 to 0.28 versus above 0.40 in the U.S. (Björklund et al., IZA). The more equal the starting line, the more individual differences, not family background, decide who finishes where. Equal opportunity does not erase unequal outcomes. It makes them more about the individual.

Four forces drive differences even under fair rules: choices, effort, talent, and luck. A free society can soften the effects of luck. It should not try to cancel choice and effort, because those are what freedom is for.

The Honest Caveat: America's Starting Line Isn't Straight

The same evidence also limits my point. If family background shapes American earnings more than it does in Denmark or Sweden, then the American starting line is less level than Americans like to believe. Mazumder found family and community matter most for those who start at the bottom. And Chicago Fed researchers found the correlation between brothers' outcomes rose substantially between men entering the workforce in the 1970s and those entering in the 1980s and early 1990s (Federal Reserve Bank of Chicago).

Neighborhood matters too. In the Moving to Opportunity experiment, randomly selected families in high-poverty housing projects were offered vouchers to move to lower-poverty neighborhoods. Economists Raj Chetty, Nathaniel Hendren, and Lawrence Katz found that children who moved before age 13 earned 31% more in their mid-twenties than children in the comparison group (Chetty, Hendren & Katz, NBER). Similar children, different zip codes, different futures.

So the honest position is this: unequal outcomes do not prove the game is rigged, but some come from unequal starts no child chose. That returns us to Chapter 4's principle: judge people by what they choose, not by what they were born into.

Straighten the Line, Then Let Them Run

What follows is neither the American left's slogan nor the right's. It is a division of labor. Society's job is the starting line: safe neighborhoods, decent schools, rules that apply equally to the banker and the bricklayer, and no one barred from the race by birth. Where the line is crooked, straighten it.

The individual's job is the race. Once the start is fair, the remaining gaps mostly reflect what people did with their chance, and a free country should let them stand.

Beware anyone who points at every gap and cries "rigged," and anyone who points at every gap and shrugs "they earned it." Both have stopped looking at the evidence. The thinking citizen asks each time: was this gap caused by the start, or by the running?
