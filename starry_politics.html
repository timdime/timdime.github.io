Politics, Left & Right

Tyson's perspective in Starry Messenger

From the Overture ("Science & Society") and Chapter Four ("Conflict & Resolution")

Neil deGrasse Tyson lifts politics off the "compass rose." Most left–right conflict, he argues, isn't really about the issues — it's tribalism, a Paleolithic instinct to build groups around who looks like us, prays like us, and shares our moral code. Evolution wired this in because groupthink once conferred survival, but in a world of eight billion it curdles into the in-group/out-group hostility that drives everything from social-media pile-ons to war.

His antidote is the cosmic perspective — the "overview effect" astronauts describe when they see Earth whole from orbit, borderless and small. From far enough away, he says, the planet supports not many tribes but a single human tribe, and once you argue from that vantage many disagreements soften while others simply evaporate. His model for how to argue comes from science: scientists don't seek each other's opinions, they seek each other's data, and they "allocate confidence in proportion to the strength of the evidence." He's careful, though, not to promise that rationality makes everyone agree. It can't. What it can do is make people disagree less strenuously — not through compromise, but by separating emotion from the capacity to reason.

1 · Three kinds of truth

Trouble starts when a political truth is treated as an objective one.

This three-way split is central to the book. Objective truths — Earth is round, the air is 78% nitrogen, humans are warming the planet — are established by repeated experiment and are "true even when you don't believe in them"; they don't come from any single authority or paper. Personal truths are what you hold dear but can't prove: your faith, your values, whether the government should support the poor. They're harmless "argued over a beer," but become dangerous when someone tries to legislate that everyone must share them. Political truths are the most corrosive: ideas that already resonate with your feelings and then harden into unassailable "facts" through incessant media repetition — the basic mechanism of propaganda. Tyson's warning is that the efficacy of science should never correlate with your political views, and that handpicking pre-consensus research to serve a cultural, economic, or political agenda "undermines the foundations of an informed democracy."

2 · The cosmic perspective rises above the poles

Not compromise or "centrist mush" — a vantage that separates emotion from reason.

3 · Four red–blue tropes, tested against data

Tyson flatters neither side; the stereotypes don't survive the numbers.

The four tropes are worth unpacking, because this is where Tyson most deliberately refuses to take a side. On family values, he notes that the ten states with the highest shares of out-of-wedlock births all voted red this century, nine of the ten highest-divorce states voted red in 2020, and the only two divorced presidents (Reagan and Trump) were Republicans — yet left-leaning states topped the Ashley Madison adultery leak. Neither side, he concludes, owns morality.

On science, he draws a sharp asymmetry. Conservative denial of climate change is the one position that could genuinely destabilize civilization, so it isn't equivalent to the rest. But liberals can't smugly brand themselves "pro-science" while embracing homeopathy, astrology, crystal healing, anti-GMO and anti-pharma stances, and the anti-vax movement they "practically invented" (measles outbreaks clustered in blue states before the movement turned purple). He adds a twist: by the hard measure of federal R&D funding, the supposed "science deniers" actually fund science well — Eisenhower posted the biggest increases, and Clinton and Obama the smallest.

On race and inclusion, he recounts the long historical swap — Lincoln's antislavery Republican Party versus the Democrats who ran the Jim Crow South — while insisting it's "not entirely 180 degrees": Republican presidents appointed the first two Black secretaries of state, and the partisan lines still tangle in unexpected ways. And on taxes and patriotism, the data inverts the rhetoric: eight of the ten states that pay more to Washington than they receive are blue, while several of the most federally dependent states are red.

He layers on a lesson about confirmation bias — the habit of remembering the hits and forgetting the misses. His example is personal: when his Cosmos reboot aired on Fox rather than PBS, his most left-leaning friends assumed it meant capture by Fox News, blind to the fact that the Fox portfolio also produced Avatar, 12 Years a Slave, and the pointedly progressive Simpsons and Glee. The antidote, he says, is "dispassionate rational analysis."

His own story anchors the whole argument: a self-described liberal, Tyson was twice appointed to White House commissions by George W. Bush, who wanted his scientific expertise and didn't seem to mind his politics. Working shoulder to shoulder with conservative policy wonks and progressive labor leaders, he found the productive conversations all happened in the political center — every step he took toward the conservative worldview took him that much farther from the liberal one he'd known, until he felt he was "truly thinking for myself" for the first time. He came to resent labels as "intellectually lazy ways of asserting you know everything about a person you've never met."

"I always voted at my party's call, / And I never thought of thinking for myself at all." — the trap he warns against. Let a party think for you and you become "a pawn"; in a republic, power should be the pawn of the people.

The point isn't forced compromise. It's allocating confidence in proportion to evidence — separating emotion from reason so people disagree less strenuously and preserve democracy's real virtue: we get to disagree without killing one another.

Source: Neil deGrasse Tyson, Starry Messenger: Cosmic Perspectives on Civilization — Overture and Chapter Four.
